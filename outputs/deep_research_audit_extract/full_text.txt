Audit of Predictive-Validity Collapse in the Transition from HumanEval to LiveCodeBench-v6 as of May 2, 2026
The evaluation of large language models (LLMs) has historically relied upon static benchmarks to narrate progress and rank architectural innovations. However, as these benchmarks mature, they often transition from being broad measures of general intelligence to narrow targets for optimization, leading to a phenomenon known as Goodhart’s Law, where a measure ceases to be an effective metric once it becomes a target.1 This audit focuses on the critical transition between the legacy HumanEval benchmark and the modern LiveCodeBench-v6 (LCB-v6) framework. HumanEval, consisting of 164 hand-crafted Python problems, has been a public optimization target since 2021, while LiveCodeBench-v6 utilizes problems collected from competitive programming contests through April 2025 to ensure contamination resistance.2 The central inquiry of this deep research audit is whether the rank ordering of models on HumanEval still predicts performance on the fresher, more complex problems of LCB-v6, or whether we are witnessing a complete predictive-validity collapse or even a rank inversion in the frontier model band.
Predictive validity is an external property of a benchmark that evaluates whether its induced model ordering is preserved on harder or newer transfer tasks.1 In the context of coding benchmarks, saturation occurs when models cluster above a 90% success rate, making HumanEval rankings statistically indistinguishable for selection purposes.3 This audit investigates a more severe failure mode: predictive-validity collapse, where the signed rank correlation becomes unstable or near-zero, and inversion, where the correlation becomes significantly negative.1 The evidence suggests that for frontier models released between late 2024 and mid-2026, HumanEval has largely failed as a ranking instrument, showing a strong high-band inversion in narrow cohorts and a general collapse in broader populations.1
Analysis of Frontier Benchmark Saturation and Rank Transfer
The construction of a source-verified dataset for this audit required identifying models achieving at least a 90% pass@1 rate on HumanEval and matching them with exact LCB-v6 variants. The primary challenge in benchmark auditing is the prevalence of estimated or approximate scores in secondary technical reports. To ensure the integrity of Goal A, this audit prioritized scores from primary technical disclosures or verified reproduction harnesses such as those provided by Berkeley, MIT, and Cornell researchers.4
As of May 2, 2026, the frontier of code generation is dominated by massive Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures and models utilizing extended "Thinking" or reasoning modes.7 These models frequently reach the ceiling of HumanEval, which primarily tests easy-to-medium standalone function completion.2 In contrast, LCB-v6 incorporates competition-grade problems that require complex algorithmic reasoning and multi-step planning, which often exposes the limitations of models that have over-optimized for the static HumanEval distribution.4

Performance Divergence on Hardened Benchmarks and Decontamination Probes
A critical component of this audit (Goal B) involves probing for HumanEval exposure through performance on hardened variants and analysis of training cutoffs. HumanEval+ (EvalPlus) provides a rigorous test of functional correctness by extending the original test cases by approximately 80x using a mutation-based framework.26 This hardening process often detects incorrect code that would otherwise pass the sparse HumanEval unit tests, which average only 7.7 tests per problem.10
Evidence from EvalPlus leaderboards indicates a consistent performance drop of 7 to 10 percentage points when moving from base HumanEval to HumanEval+.28 For instance, models achieving 96% on base HumanEval tests frequently drop to the high 80s under more comprehensive testing.28 This gap suggests that many models are over-optimizing for specific, static unit tests rather than demonstrating robust programming proficiency.3 Furthermore, HumanEval-T, which uses combinatorial template designs to dynamically re-instantiate problems, shows drops of 3 to 14 percentage points, highlighting potential data leakage in the original static protocol.10
The most severe degradation is observed on HumanEval Pro, which requires compositional reasoning to solve composite tasks derived from the base functions.10 Frontier models often experience a pass@1 drop of 10 to 25 points on Pro tasks, exposing a lack of compositional generalization that is hidden by the saturated scores on simpler problems.10 These results suggest that static benchmark optimization leads to "latent contamination," where selection bias and exposure to common solutions inflate scores without corresponding gains in transferable ability.29

The "Codeforces Cliff" further clarifies the mechanism of this collapse. LiveCodeBench-v6 allows for evaluation on unseen problems released after a model's training cutoff date.4 For models like DeepSeek-V3 or Claude 3.5 Sonnet, performance on problems released before 2024 is significantly higher than on problems released in early 2025, confirming that temporal generalization is the primary barrier for contaminated systems.15
Architectural and Post-Training Drivers of Benchmark Divergence
The predictive-validity collapse is not merely a statistical anomaly but is rooted in major shifts in model architecture and training regimes observed between 2025 and 2026. The frontier has transitioned toward massive Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) designs, such as the 1.6T parameter DeepSeek-V4-Pro and the 1T parameter Kimi K2.6.8 These models utilize advanced post-training techniques, including reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) and thinking modes, which dramatically improve performance on complex, long-horizon tasks like those in LCB-v6 and SWE-bench Verified while causing HumanEval scores to reach near-ceiling saturation.9
DeepSeek-V4-Pro features a hybrid attention stack—Compressed Sparse Attention (CSA) and Heavily Compressed Attention (HCA)—that enables tractable 1 million token context windows.8 Its use of the Muon optimizer at scale has been reported to improve convergence and stability, allowing it to achieve a state-of-the-art 93.5% on LCB-v6.8 Similarly, Kimi K2.6 utilizes an Agent Swarm orchestration layer to execute thousands of coordinated steps, achieving 89.6% on LCB-v6 and 80.2% on SWE-bench Verified.9 These architectural strengths are precisely what older function-completion benchmarks fail to differentiate.
Furthermore, the introduction of "Thinking" budgets has decoupled raw parametric knowledge from reasoning depth. Models like Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.6 Thinking show significantly higher scores on LCB-v6 when allowed to engage in extended chain-of-thought processing.7 For example, DeepSeek-R1-0528 improved its LCB-v6 score from 63.5% to 73.3% through reasoning upgrades alone, a delta that is virtually invisible on HumanEval due to the simplicity of its problem set.35

Audit Synthesis and Manuscript Edit Recommendations
The results of this audit demonstrate that HumanEval is no longer a valid predictive instrument for frontier model selection. The combination of saturated scores, high pairwise inversion rates (), and zero top-5 overlap between HumanEval and LCB-v6 indicates that leaderboard rankings on legacy static benchmarks are likely driven by target-specific exposure rather than underlying coding capability.1 The broader population audit confirms that while the benchmark remains a useful coarse filter for distinguishing between "toy" models and "competent" models, it fails to differentiate between the various levels of "competent" frontier systems.1
For practitioners, the implication is clear: model selection for coding tasks must be based on contamination-resistant, dynamic benchmarks like LiveCodeBench or repository-scale evaluations like SWE-bench Verified. Rankings clustered near the HumanEval ceiling should be treated as statistically uninformative.3 For researchers and benchmark maintainers, the following manuscript edit recommendations are proposed to align scientific reporting with the observed reality of predictive-validity collapse.
Recommendation 1: Population-Specific Validity Reporting
The manuscript should replace universal "benchmark health" claims with population-dependent diagnostics. A benchmark may be "healthy" for general model comparison while being "inverted" for frontier selection.
Proposed Edit: "We flag a predictive-validity inversion when  and the pairwise inversion rate exceeds 60% in the target population cohort. For the 2025-2026 frontier cohort (), HumanEval exhibits a strong inversion (), whereas it remains a coarse positive predictor () for a broader historical population including mid-tier models." 1
Recommendation 2: Mechanistic Attribution and Compression
The paper should explicitly state that compression alone is insufficient to explain the collapse, as control tests using MMLU distributions failed to replicate the inversion.
Proposed Edit: "Mechanism-discriminant analysis suggests that HumanEval's failure is driven by construct mismatch and exposure rather than mere score compression. Imposing HumanEval-like compression on the MMLU leaderboard preserved a positive correlation with GPQA Diamond (), whereas integer-quantizing the original HumanEval slice left its LCB-v6 relation strongly negative ()." 1
Recommendation 3: Standardizing "Thinking" vs "Standard" Benchmarks
The transition results show that "Thinking" modes significantly alter the predictive relationship. These should be reported as distinct model variants to prevent rank noise.
Proposed Edit: "Evaluations must distinguish between 'Reasoning' (Thinking) and 'Standard' (Non-thinking) modes. Mixing these modes in rank transfer audits conflates parametric knowledge with test-time compute, masking the underlying decay of static benchmark validity." 33
Recommendation 4: Mandatory Transfer Validity Audits
New benchmarks should be required to publish overlap tables and rank transfer statistics as part of their technical release.
Proposed Edit: "A mature benchmark audit protocol should include Spearman  with bootstrap intervals, pairwise inversion rates, and top-k Jaccard overlap relative to a recognized transfer task. A benchmark should be considered for retirement once its signed rank validity collapses () or inverts for frontier system selection." 1
Final Conclusions of the Audit
The deep research audit as of May 2, 2026, confirms a systemic predictive-validity collapse in the HumanEval to LiveCodeBench-v6 transition. For models achieving scores of 90% or higher on HumanEval, the leaderboard has lost its ranking utility. In narrow frontier cohorts, this manifests as a rank inversion, where high-scoring HumanEval models are statistically outmatched on newer, harder problems by models with "lower" HumanEval scores but superior architectural reasoning depth.1
The transition from static to dynamic evaluation is a necessary response to the maturation of LLMs. As models move toward trillion-parameter MoE architectures and agentic swarms, benchmarks must evolve from standalone function completion to holistic algorithmic problem-solving. This audit serves as a warning that relying on stale leaderboards leads to significant model-selection errors. The "Codeforces Cliff" and performance decay on hardened benchmarks like EvalPlus and HumanEval Pro provide conclusive evidence that static scores are increasingly decoupled from transferable capability. Moving forward, a multidimensional audit protocol—incorporating threshold sensitivity, version control, and provenance flags—is essential for maintaining evaluation integrity in the era of saturated frontier models.
